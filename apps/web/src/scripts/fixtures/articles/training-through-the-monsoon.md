The monsoon brings relief from the summer heat and a long list of new problems. Roads flood, tiles turn slick, shoes never dry, and the mosquitoes arrive. Many runners lose two or three months of training each year to the rains. That is a shame, because the cooler air can make the monsoon a good time to build fitness for the races later in the year. With a few adjustments, you can keep going.

## Footing comes first

Most monsoon running injuries are not overuse injuries. They are falls. Wet surfaces lose their grip in ways that are easy to forget:

- **Painted road markings, metal covers, and smooth tiles** become very slippery when wet. Step around them, especially on corners.
- **Algae and moss** grow quickly on footpaths, promenades, and shaded stretches that stay damp. A path that was fine in April can be treacherous by July.
- **Standing water hides what is underneath.** A puddle can cover a pothole, a broken slab, or an open drain. If you cannot see the bottom, go around it.
- **Mud on trails** changes how your foot lands. Shorten your stride, slow down, and keep your feet under your body.

Run a little slower than usual, especially downhill and on turns. A shorter, quicker stride keeps you more stable on slippery ground. Wet roads also mean poorer visibility and longer braking distances for drivers, so wear something bright or reflective, run facing traffic where there is no footpath, and be extra careful at junctions.

If there is thunder and lightning, stop running outdoors and find proper shelter in a building. Do not shelter under an isolated tree.

## Shoes, socks, and chafing

Wet shoes are heavier, they rub more, and they take a long time to dry in humid air.

- **Grip matters more than cushioning now.** If your shoes have worn smooth, the rains will show you. A shoe with a grippier rubber outsole or a light trail shoe can help on wet surfaces.
- **Dry your shoes properly.** Take out the insoles, loosen the laces, and stuff the shoes with newspaper, changing it once or twice. Leave them somewhere airy. Avoid putting them on a heater or in strong direct sun, which can damage the foam and glue.
- **Rotate pairs if you can.** Two pairs that dry out between runs last longer and smell better than one pair that is always damp.
- **Choose synthetic or wool socks**, not cotton. Cotton holds water and is a common cause of blisters in the rain. Some runners prefer thinner socks that hold less water.

Chafing gets worse when skin and clothing are wet for a long time. The usual places are the inner thighs, underarms, the chest and nipples, and under bra straps and waistbands. Put an anti-chafing balm or petroleum jelly on these areas before every run. Wear fitted synthetic clothing that does not bunch. After the run, wash, dry yourself fully, and change out of wet clothes quickly. Damp skin for hours on end also invites fungal infections between the toes and in skin folds, so dry your feet carefully.

## When it is not safe to go out

Some days the right call is not to run outdoors. That includes heavy rain with poor visibility, flooded roads, lightning, and official warnings from your city or the weather department. Missing a run in these conditions is not a failure of discipline. It is good judgement.

Have a plan ready so a bad day does not become a lost week:

- **Treadmill.** If you have access to one, it is the simplest substitute. Set a slight incline if it helps the effort feel like road running.
- **Stairs.** Many apartment buildings have long stairwells. Walking or running up and walking down is excellent work for the legs and heart. Hold the rail on the way down.
- **Strength and mobility.** A 30 to 40 minute session of squats, lunges, step-ups, calf raises, hip work, and core exercises supports your running and needs only floor space.
- **Cross-training.** Cycling on a stationary bike, swimming once the pools are open, or brisk walking in a covered space all keep your fitness ticking over.

## Keeping consistency

The monsoon tends to break routines. A run is missed because of rain, then another, and soon a month has passed. A few habits help:

- **Decide the night before.** Check the forecast and pick either the outdoor plan or the indoor one, so there is no decision to make in the morning.
- **Think in weeks, not days.** If Tuesday is washed out, move the session to Wednesday. What matters is the shape of your week, not the exact day each run falls on.
- **Shorten rather than skip.** A 20 minute run between showers is far better than nothing.
- **Accept slower times.** Wet roads, heavy shoes, and careful footing make you slower. That is fine.
- **Run with others.** Club group runs and running partners make it much easier to get out of the door on a grey morning.

If you have an ERC coach, mention the weather in your weekly check-in. Your coach can swap sessions around a bad forecast so the week still adds up.

## Dengue and other monsoon illnesses

The monsoon is also the main season for mosquito-borne illness, and runners are outdoors at exactly the times the mosquitoes are active.

**Dengue** is spread by Aedes mosquitoes, which mostly bite during the day, with peaks in the early morning and late afternoon. They breed in small pools of standing water. Sensible precautions:

- Use an insect repellent on exposed skin before morning and evening runs.
- Consider light, long sleeves on routes through parks and greenery.
- Do not leave water standing in pots, buckets, trays, or old tyres at home, and empty the tray under your water cooler.

**Leptospirosis** is a bacterial infection that can spread through floodwater contaminated by animal urine, especially through cuts and grazes on the feet and legs. Avoid running or wading through floodwater. If you do get soaked in it, wash well with soap and clean water afterwards and cover any cuts.

**Stomach infections** are more common when water supplies are affected. Drink from sources you trust on runs, and carry your own water if you are not sure.

**Fever means rest.** Do not run with a fever. Running while ill does not build fitness, and it can make you more unwell. When the fever has gone and you feel normal, start back with a few short, easy runs before returning to your usual training.

## See a doctor if

See a doctor promptly if you develop a fever during the monsoon, particularly with a headache, pain behind the eyes, body or joint aches, a rash, or vomiting. Tell them if you have been in floodwater. Until a doctor has assessed you, avoid taking painkillers such as ibuprofen or aspirin for a monsoon fever, as these are not advised if it turns out to be dengue. Seek urgent care for severe stomach pain, repeated vomiting, bleeding from the gums or nose, or unusual drowsiness. After a fall, get medical advice for any pain that stops you walking normally, swelling that does not settle, or a head injury.

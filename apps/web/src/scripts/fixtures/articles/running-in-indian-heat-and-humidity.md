For much of the year, most of India is hot, and for a good part of it, humid as well. You cannot train around that, so you learn to train with it. Heat does not make running dangerous on its own. Running hard in heat you are not ready for, without enough fluid, and ignoring the early warning signs is what gets runners into trouble. This article covers the habits our coaches ask every runner to build.

## Why heat makes running harder

Running produces a lot of heat inside the body. You lose most of it by sweating, and the sweat cools you only when it evaporates from your skin. On a dry day, that works well. On a humid morning in Chennai, Mumbai, or Kolkata, the air is already full of moisture, so sweat drips off instead of evaporating, and you cool far less for the same amount of sweat.

Your heart is also doing two jobs at once. It is sending blood to your working muscles and to your skin to shed heat. The result is that the same pace feels harder and your heart rate sits higher than it would on a cool day. That is normal. It is not a sign that you have lost fitness.

The practical lesson is simple. **In heat, run by effort, not by pace.** If your easy runs are usually at a pace where you can hold a conversation, keep that conversation test and let the pace slow down. A slower easy run in May is the same training as a faster one in December.

## Timing your runs

The coolest part of the day is just before sunrise. In most Indian cities, the hour between first light and sunrise is the best window you will get, and the sun gains strength quickly after that. Late evening is the second choice, but roads, tracks, and concrete hold heat long after sunset, so an evening run can feel warmer than the air temperature suggests.

A few habits help:

- Plan hard sessions and long runs for the earliest start you can manage, and keep the hottest months for easier, shorter work.
- Choose shaded routes, parks, and loops that pass a tap, a shop, or your own front door so you can refill.
- Wear light-coloured, loose, synthetic clothing and a cap or visor. Cotton holds sweat and gets heavy.
- Use sunscreen on runs that go past sunrise.
- On the worst days, move the session indoors, shorten it, or swap it for an easy run. Missing one hard session costs you very little. Heat illness can cost you weeks.

## Hydration: drink to thirst, start well

Most of our runners do not need a complicated hydration plan for runs of under an hour. What matters more is starting the run already well hydrated. Drink steadily through the day before a morning run, and have a glass of water when you wake up.

During longer runs, drink when you are thirsty. Thirst is a reasonable guide for most healthy runners in training. Carry a bottle, wear a belt, or plan a route past places where you can drink.

Drinking more is not always better. Forcing down large amounts of plain water beyond thirst, especially over several hours, can dilute the sodium in your blood. This condition, called hyponatraemia, is uncommon but serious, and it is more likely in slower runners on long efforts who drink at every opportunity. Drink to thirst, and do not try to gain weight during a run.

Two simple checks help you learn your own needs:

- **Urine colour.** Pale straw is a good sign. Dark yellow on waking suggests you finished yesterday short of fluid.
- **Weighing yourself.** Weigh before and after a run of an hour or more, with minimal clothing and after towelling off. A loss of one kilogram is roughly one litre of sweat you did not replace. If you regularly lose a lot, you need to drink more on the run and afterwards. If you ever finish heavier than you started, you drank too much.

## Electrolytes

Sweat carries salt, mostly sodium, as well as water. Some people sweat much more salt than others. If you see white salt marks on your cap or T-shirt after a run, you are probably one of them.

For runs under an hour, water and your normal meals are enough for most people. For runs longer than about an hour, on very hot days, or if you are a heavy or salty sweater, add electrolytes. Oral rehydration salts, electrolyte tablets, sports drinks, salted nimbu pani, or buttermilk with salt all do the job. You do not need an expensive brand. After a long run, a normal meal with some salt in it replaces much of what you lost.

## Heat acclimatisation

Your body adapts to heat. With repeated exposure, you start sweating earlier, your sweat becomes less salty, your blood volume rises, and the same run in the same heat begins to feel easier. Most of this adaptation happens over about one to two weeks of regular training in the heat, and it fades if you spend several weeks away from it.

This matters in a few situations:

- **Coming back from a break.** After an illness, a holiday, or a few weeks off, treat the heat as new again.
- **Travelling to race.** A runner from Bengaluru racing in Chennai, or a runner coming back from a cool trip abroad, is not adapted to the heat at the start line.
- **The start of summer.** The first hot weeks of the year are when many runners struggle. Shorten runs and lower the effort, then build back over a couple of weeks.

Acclimatisation helps, but it does not make you immune to heat illness. Keep the warning signs in mind all year round.

## Warning signs of heat illness

Know these signs, in yourself and in the people you run with.

**Heat exhaustion** often comes on gradually. Watch for heavy sweating, pale or clammy skin, a headache, dizziness, nausea, muscle cramps, unusual tiredness, and a fast, weak pulse. If you notice any of these, stop running. Get into shade or air conditioning, lie down with your legs raised, loosen clothing, sip water or an electrolyte drink, and cool your skin with water and fanning. Most people improve within about half an hour. If you do not, get medical help.

**Heat stroke** is a medical emergency. The key warning sign is a change in the brain: confusion, odd behaviour, slurred speech, stumbling, aggression, or collapse. The skin may be hot, and the runner may or may not still be sweating. Call for an ambulance straight away on 112, or your local ambulance number, which is 108 in many states. While you wait, start cooling the runner as fast as you can. Immerse them in cool water if possible, or soak them with water and fan them, and apply ice or cold packs to the neck, armpits, and groin. Fast cooling matters more than anything else you can do.

## Building the habits

None of this needs special equipment. It needs a few habits, repeated:

- Check the weather the night before and set your alarm to beat the sun.
- Start every run well hydrated, carry water for anything over an hour, and add electrolytes for long or very sweaty runs.
- Judge easy runs by effort and breathing, not by your watch.
- Ease into the heat after a break or a trip.
- Stop at the first warning sign. There is always another run.

If you have a coach at ERC, tell them in your weekly check-in when the heat is getting to you. A coach can move your sessions around the hottest weeks so your training keeps progressing without the risk.

## See a doctor if

Speak to a doctor before training hard in heat if you have a heart condition, high blood pressure, diabetes, or kidney disease, if you are pregnant, or if you take medication that affects fluid balance or heart rate, such as diuretics or beta blockers. Get medical help if symptoms of heat exhaustion do not improve with rest and cooling within about 30 minutes. Treat any confusion, collapse, or slurred speech after running in heat as an emergency.

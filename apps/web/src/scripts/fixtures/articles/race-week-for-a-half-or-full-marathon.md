You have done the training. The long runs are behind you, and the race is a few days away. Race week cannot make you much fitter, but it can easily make you more tired, more anxious, or more sore than you need to be. The aim this week is to arrive at the start line rested, fed, calm, and with no surprises. This article walks through the week for a half or full marathon, with the very early start times common at Indian winter races, such as the Mumbai Marathon in January, in mind.

## The taper: less running, not no running

Most marathon plans reduce training over the last two to three weeks, and a half marathon usually needs one to two. This is called the taper. Its job is to let the fatigue from months of training clear while you keep the fitness you have built.

In race week itself:

- **Cut the volume.** Run noticeably less than in a normal week, with shorter runs and perhaps one fewer run.
- **Keep a little intensity.** A short session early in the week, such as a few minutes at race pace or some strides, keeps your legs feeling sharp. Do not try to prove your fitness. The work is done.
- **Rest the day or two before.** A full rest day, or a 15 to 20 minute jog with a few strides, is plenty.

It is very common to feel sluggish, restless, or oddly achy during a taper. Your body is recovering, and with less running, you notice small things you would normally ignore. This is normal and usually passes by race day. Resist the urge to squeeze in an extra run to reassure yourself.

## Food: familiar, simple, and enough carbohydrate

For a half marathon, eat your normal, balanced diet through the week and make sure your meals the day before include plenty of carbohydrate. For a full marathon, it is worth going further and making carbohydrate a larger share of what you eat over the last two to three days. Your muscles store carbohydrate as glycogen, and topping up those stores before a race lasting several hours helps you hold your pace later on.

In practice, that means more rice, roti, idli, dosa, poha, upma, bread, potatoes, and fruit, and a little less of the very fibrous, fatty, or heavy foods that sit in your stomach. You will probably gain a little weight as your muscles store carbohydrate and water. That is expected.

Keep to food you know. Race week is not the time for a new restaurant, a street-food adventure, very spicy dishes, or a supplement you have never tried. If you are travelling, think ahead about where you will eat the evening before and on race morning, especially for a start before dawn when nothing near your hotel may be open.

Drink normally through the week. There is no need to force down extra water. Pale straw urine is a fair sign you are well hydrated.

## Sleep

Prioritise sleep in the days leading up to the race. Go to bed a little earlier from early in the week and keep screens out of the last half hour before bed.

Almost everyone sleeps badly the night before a big race. Nerves, an alarm set for 3 am, and a strange hotel bed all play a part. Do not worry about it. Many experienced runners find that a few good nights earlier in the week matter more than the final one, and a single short night rarely spoils a race. Lie down, rest, and let it be.

With very early starts, it helps to shift your routine gradually. If you will need to wake at 3 or 3:30 am, start going to bed and getting up a little earlier from a few days before.

## Kit check

Lay everything out two days before the race, not the night before. That leaves time to fix anything that is missing.

- Race bib, safety pins, and timing chip if it is separate. Read the organiser's instructions on where the chip goes.
- The shoes you have trained in, with some kilometres already on them. Never race a marathon in brand new shoes.
- Socks, shorts, and top you have worn on long runs without chafing.
- Anti-chafing balm or petroleum jelly, and plasters for any spot that rubs.
- Your race nutrition: the same gels, chews, or snacks you practised with on long runs, and electrolytes if you use them.
- Watch charged, and earphones if the race permits them.
- An old T-shirt or a bin bag to keep warm at the start and throw away, and dry clothes and a snack for your bag drop.
- Your ID, some cash, and your phone, with the route to the start already saved.

## The day before

- Collect your bib at the expo if you have not already. Do not spend hours walking around it.
- Keep off your feet as much as you can.
- Eat your main carbohydrate meal at lunch or early in the evening, so it has time to settle.
- Check the start time, the start location, road closures, and how you will get there. Early race-day transport often runs differently from normal days.
- Pin your bib to your top, set two alarms, and lay out your kit by the door.

## Race morning

For a start before sunrise, work backwards from the gun time.

- **Wake up about three hours before the start.** This gives you time to eat, digest, and use the toilet before you leave.
- **Eat a light breakfast you have tested** before long runs, two to three hours before the start. Bread or toast with banana and honey, poha, idli, or a bowl of oats are common choices. Keep it low in fibre and fat.
- **Sip water or an electrolyte drink** in small amounts, and stop drinking large amounts in the last hour so you are not looking for a toilet at the start.
- **Arrive at the start area with time to spare.** Queues for toilets and bag drop at big races can be long.
- **Warm up gently.** For a full marathon, a few minutes of walking and very easy jogging is enough. For a half, add a few minutes of easy running and some strides.

## Pacing the race

Start slower than you want to. The first few kilometres of a marathon should feel almost too easy. Stick to the pace you trained for, not the pace you hope for on a good day. Drink at aid stations when you are thirsty, take your nutrition on the schedule you practised, and remember that the sun rises during the race, so later kilometres will be warmer than the start.

## After the finish

Keep walking, collect your medal, drink, and eat something within an hour or so. Change into dry clothes. Expect stiffness for a few days. Easy walking helps. Take at least a few days off running after a half marathon and a week or more after a full, and come back with short, easy runs.

## See a doctor if

Do not race with a fever, a chest infection, or a stomach bug, however much the entry cost. Stop running and get medical help at once from the race medical team for chest pain or tightness, fainting or near-fainting, confusion, or unusual breathlessness. Get medical advice after the race for pain that makes you limp or does not settle within a few days, very dark urine, or passing very little urine.

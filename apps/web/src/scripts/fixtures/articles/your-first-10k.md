Ten kilometres is a wonderful distance for a first race. It is long enough to feel like a real achievement and short enough that you can prepare for it in a couple of months without training taking over your life. This article gives you the shape of eight weeks of training and a plan for race day. It is a shape, not a rigid plan. Adjust it to your life, and talk to a coach if you want it tailored to you.

## Before you start

This outline assumes you can already run for about 30 minutes without stopping, at an easy pace, three times a week. If you cannot yet, spend a few more weeks getting there first, using run and walk intervals. There is no rush. A 10K will still be there in two months.

You also need a rough sense of what "easy" means. An easy run is one where you can speak in full sentences. You should finish feeling you could have kept going. Most new runners run their easy days too fast, and that is the most common reason a first training block ends in tiredness or a niggle.

## The three ingredients

Each week has the same three ingredients.

**Easy runs.** Two runs a week, usually 25 to 40 minutes, at conversational effort. These build the base of your fitness: stronger heart, stronger legs, and tissues that cope better with impact. Most of your running should feel like this.

**One longer run.** Once a week, usually at the weekend, you run a little longer than your other runs, still at an easy effort. It builds endurance and teaches you to stay relaxed for longer. By the end of the eight weeks, it should be close to the time you expect the race to take.

**One quality session.** Once a week, you run some of the time a little faster. This teaches your body to run at a stronger effort and makes race pace feel familiar. It is not a race, and it should never leave you wrecked.

Start every quality session with 10 minutes of easy running and finish with 5 to 10 minutes of easy running or walking.

## The eight weeks

The times below are for the main part of each run. Keep easy runs at a conversational effort throughout.

- **Week 1.** Two easy runs of 25 to 30 minutes. Long run of 35 minutes. Quality: 6 strides of about 20 seconds after an easy run. Strides are smooth accelerations to a fast but relaxed pace, with a full walk back between each one.
- **Week 2.** Two easy runs of 30 minutes. Long run of 40 minutes. Quality: a gentle fartlek, alternating 1 minute at a stronger effort with 2 minutes easy, six times.
- **Week 3.** Two easy runs of 30 to 35 minutes. Long run of 45 minutes. Quality: 5 repeats of 2 minutes at a strong but controlled effort, with 2 minutes of easy jogging between.
- **Week 4.** A lighter week to absorb the work. Two easy runs of 25 to 30 minutes. Long run of 40 minutes. Quality: strides only.
- **Week 5.** Two easy runs of 35 minutes. Long run of 50 to 55 minutes. Quality: 4 repeats of 4 minutes at about the effort you hope to race at, with 2 minutes easy between.
- **Week 6.** Two easy runs of 35 to 40 minutes. Long run of 60 minutes. Quality: 3 repeats of 6 minutes at race effort, with 2 minutes easy between.
- **Week 7.** Two easy runs of 35 minutes. Long run of 60 to 70 minutes, the longest of the block. Quality: 2 repeats of 10 minutes at race effort, with 3 minutes easy between.
- **Week 8, race week.** Two short easy runs of 20 to 30 minutes early in the week, one of them with 4 strides. Rest or a very short jog the day before. Race at the weekend.

Rest days matter as much as running days. Put at least one, and preferably two, full rest days in every week. If you enjoy it, a short session of strength exercises such as squats, lunges, calf raises, and planks once or twice a week helps your legs cope with the extra running.

## When life gets in the way

Weeks rarely go to plan. Here is how to bend the outline without breaking it.

- **Miss one run?** Let it go. Do not cram it into the next day.
- **Miss a whole week?** Repeat the previous week rather than jumping ahead.
- **Feeling run down?** Turn the quality session into an easy run. Fitness comes from consistent weeks, not from any single session.
- **A niggle that gets worse as you run?** Stop and rest. Pain that changes how you run is a reason to stop, not to push through.
- **Hot weather?** Run early and slow down. See our article on running in Indian heat and humidity.

## Pacing race day

Most first 10K races are won or lost in the first two kilometres. Excitement, the crowd, and fresh legs make the opening feel easy, and many runners go off far too fast and pay for it later.

A simple plan works well:

- **Kilometres 1 to 2.** Hold back on purpose. Run at an effort a little easier than your race-effort sessions felt. Let people pass you. You will see many of them again.
- **Kilometres 3 to 7.** Settle into race effort, the effort you practised in weeks 5 to 7. It should feel strong but steady. You can speak a few words, not full sentences.
- **Kilometres 8 to 10.** If you feel good, gradually push. Save your hardest effort for the last kilometre.

If you have a watch, use it to stop yourself going too fast early, not to chase a number. On a hot or humid morning, aim for even effort rather than even pace, and expect to be slower.

## The days before and the morning of

- **Wear what you trained in.** No new shoes, new clothes, or new breakfast on race day.
- **Collect your bib early** and pin it on the night before. Lay out your kit, including your shoes, socks, watch, and anything to carry.
- **Eat a familiar, simple meal** the evening before, and a light breakfast two to three hours before the start, something you have already eaten before a long run.
- **Drink normally** in the days before. Have some water on the morning, and sip at water stations if you are thirsty.
- **Arrive early.** Leave time for parking, toilets, and a gentle 5 to 10 minute warm-up jog.

## After the finish

Keep walking for a few minutes, drink something, and eat within the hour or so after. Take two or three easy days or rest days before any hard running. Then think about what you enjoyed, and what you would like to try next.

## See a doctor if

Speak to a doctor before you start if you have a heart condition, chest pain on exertion, high blood pressure, diabetes, or any other medical condition, or if you are returning to exercise after a long break. Stop running and get medical advice for chest pain, fainting, unusual breathlessness, or a pain that makes you limp or does not settle with a few days of rest.

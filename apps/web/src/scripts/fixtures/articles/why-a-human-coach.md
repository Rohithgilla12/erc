You can find a free training plan for almost any race in a few seconds. You can ask an app or a chatbot to write one for you, with your goal time and the number of days you can run. Many of those plans are perfectly sensible on paper. So why does ERC put human coaches at the centre of the club?

Because the plan is the easy part. The hard part is the twelve or sixteen weeks of real life that happen while you follow it. A plan cannot see those weeks. A coach can.

## What a plan does well, and where it stops

A good generic plan gets the basics right. It builds distance gradually, mixes easy running with some harder sessions, includes a long run, and eases off before the race. If you are healthy, consistent, and your life is predictable, a generic plan can carry you a long way.

The trouble is that a plan is written once, before any of the training happens. Even a plan generated from your answers to a few questions only knows what you told it on that one day. It does not know what happened in week three. It cannot tell the difference between a runner who missed Tuesday's session because of a work deadline and one who missed it because their knee hurt. It treats both weeks the same, and it carries on.

## What a coach notices

**Your injury history.** A coach who knows you have had shin pain before will build your running more gently, watch the weeks where your distance jumps, and notice when you mention your shins again. A plan treats every runner as if they have never been hurt.

**Your life load.** Training is only one kind of stress. A deadline at work, a sick child, a family wedding, exams, travel, a long commute, or a festival week all take energy from the same place. A coach will often cut a week back before you have asked, because they know what is coming up in your life.

**Your sleep and recovery.** Poor sleep changes how you cope with training. If you tell your coach you have been sleeping badly for a fortnight, the right response might be an easier week, not a harder one. A plan does not know.

**How you describe things.** Two runners can both say a session felt "fine". One means it was comfortable, the other means they barely got through it. Over weeks, a coach learns what your words mean.

**Patterns over time.** A single tired week is nothing. Three tired weeks in a row, with a niggle that keeps coming back, is a pattern that deserves a change of plan, and perhaps a visit to a doctor or physiotherapist. Coaches look for those patterns.

**When to hold back, and when to push.** Sometimes you are ready for more than the plan offers. Sometimes you need less. Most runners are not good at judging this for themselves, especially before a race they care about. A coach who knows you can make that call with you.

**Encouragement.** A message from someone who has followed your training for months, after a hard race or a disappointing week, means something a notification cannot.

## How the coaching loop works at ERC

ERC's coaching is built around a simple weekly rhythm between you and your coach.

**1. Ask for a coach.** From the Coaching page in your account, you can see the club coaches who are taking athletes and request one. Your coach accepts the request before anything is shared. Either of you can end the coaching relationship later.

**2. Log your runs.** The runs you record on the site are what your coach sees each week: distances, times, and any notes you add to a run.

**3. Check in each week.** On the weekly check-in page, you tell your coach three things:

- How your training felt this week: great, good, tired, struggling, or injured.
- Any niggles, aches, or tightness, in a sentence or two.
- Notes or questions for your coach, such as a bad night's sleep, a busy week at work, or a session that went well.

It takes a couple of minutes. You can update it during the week if something changes. Your check-ins are for you and your coach. They are not shown on the public site or in the club's admin screens.

**4. Your coach reviews the week.** Your coach sees your week in one place: total distance and time, the number of runs, how this week compares with the last one, your runs, and your check-in.

The site also flags a few things worth a closer look. These are simple rules, not judgements: your weekly distance going up by more than ten per cent on the week before, running all seven days with no rest day, a long run that makes up more than 40 per cent of the week, a check-in where you felt tired or struggling, a niggle you reported, or a week with no runs logged. When a flag appears, the site suggests some things to watch and some recommendations.

**5. Your coach writes your review.** This is where the human part matters. The suggestions are a starting point. Your coach reads them alongside everything they know about you, then keeps, rewrites, or deletes them, and adds their own. The review has three parts: things to watch next week, recommendations for next week, and a personal note.

**6. You read it.** When your coach publishes the review, it appears in the Coaching section of your account. Then the next week begins, and the loop starts again.

## Why the loop matters more than the plan

The value is not in any single review. It is in the repetition. Week after week, your coach builds a picture of how you respond to training: which sessions suit you, how quickly you recover, what tends to come before a niggle, and what your life looks like in a busy month. By the time you reach a race, your coach knows you as a runner far better than any form could.

That picture depends on you. A check-in that says "good" every week, whatever happened, gives your coach nothing to work with. Be honest. Mention the bad sleep, the stressful week, the ache that is probably nothing. Nothing you write is a test. It is information that helps your coach help you.

## What a coach is not

An ERC coach is an experienced runner and volunteer who helps you train well. A coach is not a doctor or a physiotherapist, and cannot diagnose an injury or an illness. When something sounds like more than a training problem, a good coach will tell you to get it checked, and will adjust your training while you do.

## See a doctor if

Tell your coach about any injury or health condition, and see a doctor or physiotherapist for pain that keeps coming back, pain that changes how you run, or any chest pain, fainting, or unusual breathlessness during exercise. Your coach will plan around what they advise.

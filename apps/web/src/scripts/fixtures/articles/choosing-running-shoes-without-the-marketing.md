Running shoes are sold with a lot of big words: energy return, propulsion, guidance, stability, super foam. Prices keep climbing, and every brand releases a new version every year. It is easy to believe the right shoe will make you faster or keep you injury-free. The truth is simpler and cheaper. A good running shoe is one that fits you well and feels comfortable when you run in it. This article explains how to find one, and what the technology does and does not do.

## Start with fit

Fit is the one thing nobody can judge for you from a website. Try shoes on in person when you can, or buy from a seller with an easy return policy.

- **Shop later in the day.** Feet swell during the day and while running, so shoes that fit in the morning can feel tight on a long run.
- **Wear your running socks**, and bring any insoles or orthotics you use.
- **Leave room at the toes.** You should have about a thumb's width between your longest toe and the end of the shoe. Your longest toe may not be your big toe.
- **Check the width.** The widest part of your foot should sit comfortably at the widest part of the shoe without pressing on the sides. Many brands make wide versions of popular models, so ask for one if the standard fit pinches.
- **Your heel should not slip.** A little movement is fine, but your heel should not lift out with each step.
- **Size varies between brands and even between models.** Ignore the number on the box and go by how the shoe fits.

Then run in them, not just walk around the shop. Many stores let you jog outside or on a treadmill. A few minutes of running tells you far more than standing in front of a mirror.

## Comfort is a good guide

When you put on a few different pairs and run in each, one usually feels better than the others: smoother, more natural, nothing rubbing or pressing. That feeling of comfort is a reasonable way to choose. Research on running shoes has generally supported letting runners choose what feels comfortable, rather than prescribing a shoe from a measurement.

For many years, shoe shops matched runners to shoes by looking at their arch or watching how much their foot rolled inwards, called pronation, and recommending "stability" or "motion control" shoes accordingly. Studies that tested this approach have not found good evidence that it prevents injuries. It is not harmful, and some runners genuinely prefer a stability shoe, but you do not need a gait analysis to buy a good pair. Let comfort lead.

## What cushioning does and does not do

Cushioning changes how a shoe feels. A soft, thick midsole feels protective and can make long runs and recovery runs more pleasant, especially on hard roads. Many runners like more cushioning as their runs get longer.

What cushioning has not been shown to do reliably is reduce injuries. Your body adjusts how it lands to the surface and shoe underneath it, and research has not found that more cushioning consistently means fewer injuries. Choose the level of cushioning you enjoy running in. There is no single right amount.

## What carbon plates and super shoes do and do not do

The racing shoes that arrived in the late 2010s combine a very light, bouncy foam with a stiff plate, often carbon fibre, running through the sole. Lab studies have consistently shown that these shoes help many runners use less energy at the same pace, and race times at the elite level have fallen since they appeared. They are real technology, not just marketing.

There are some important qualifications:

- **The benefit varies from runner to runner**, and some people gain little. The research has mostly involved faster runners at faster speeds.
- **They are less stable.** The tall, soft foam can feel wobbly, especially on corners, uneven roads, and tired legs.
- **They can load the body differently.** Some runners notice more strain in their calves, Achilles, or feet. Introduce them gradually.
- **They wear out faster** and cost a lot. Most runners keep them for races and a few key sessions, and train in ordinary shoes.
- **They do not replace training.** A plated shoe on an under-prepared runner is still an under-prepared runner.

If you are new to running, you do not need one. Buy a comfortable everyday trainer and spend the difference on a race entry.

## Rotating pairs

Many experienced runners own two or three pairs and alternate them. A common pattern is a cushioned pair for easy and long runs, and a lighter pair for faster sessions and races. There is some research suggesting runners who rotate shoes are injured less often, though it is not settled. The practical benefits are clearer. Each pair gets time to dry out, which matters a lot in the Indian monsoon, and your shoes wear out more slowly because each one is used less.

If you are just starting out, one good pair is fine. Add a second when your running becomes regular.

## When to replace a shoe

You will often hear that a running shoe lasts 500 to 800 kilometres. Treat that as a rough rule of thumb, not a scientific limit. How long a shoe lasts depends on your weight, how you run, the surfaces you run on, and how the shoe is made. A light runner on smooth roads may get much more. A heavier runner on rough ground may get less.

Look for these signs instead:

- The outsole is worn through to the foam in places, or has worn smooth and lost its grip.
- The midsole has visible deep creases, or one side has compressed more than the other so the shoe tilts on a table.
- The shoe feels flat and harsh compared with when it was new.
- The upper has torn, or the heel counter has collapsed.
- You notice new aches after runs in that pair that go away in another pair.

Keeping a note of the distance on each pair, in a running app or a notebook, helps you spot when a pair is getting old.

## Making shoes last

- Use your running shoes only for running, not for walking around all day.
- Loosen the laces to take them off, rather than prising them off with the other foot.
- Dry them at room temperature with newspaper inside, never on a heater or in strong sun.
- Do not put them in a washing machine. Brush off mud and wipe them clean.

## The short version

Find a shoe that fits and feels comfortable when you run. Buy it in a size that suits your feet, not the number you usually wear. Keep fast, plated shoes for racing if you want them. Replace shoes when they are worn, not when a calendar says so.

## See a doctor if

Shoes rarely cause or cure injuries on their own. If you have pain that keeps coming back, a pain that makes you change how you run, numbness or tingling in your feet, or a foot problem related to diabetes, see a doctor or a physiotherapist rather than trying to solve it by changing shoes.
